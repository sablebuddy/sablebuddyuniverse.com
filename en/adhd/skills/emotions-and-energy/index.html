Emotions and energy

A colleague messages: "let's go over your part of the report". Within a second your brain has filled in the whole story: he's unhappy, I didn't deliver, I'm about to get taken apart. You write a reply, delete it, write it again.

An hour later it turns out he just wanted to check the table format.

Or another one. In the morning you got more done in two hours than you usually manage in a day. By four in the afternoon you can't assemble a five-word sentence, and you're angry at yourself, because you could this morning.

The reaction arrives before the thought, and your resources don't show up consistently

There are two mechanisms in this zone, and they're different.

The first is speed. An emotion arrives faster than you can make sense of it, and stronger than the situation warrants. You don't decide to be frightened or angry, you find yourself already frightened or angry. The explanation comes afterwards and finds the conclusion already drawn.

The second is unevenness. Your resources don't arrive in a steady stream. There in the morning, gone by afternoon; there one week of the month, gone the next; gone for a long stretch after a heavy load. Planning as if it were a constant makes no sense, and that's exactly how everyone plans.

Hence the rule for this zone: these skills don't remove the emotion and don't add energy. They buy time between the reaction and the action, and they take into account that today you don't have as much as yesterday.

Emotions all over the place

The main thing here is buying time. A wave of strong emotion doesn't last long, around a minute and a half, and if you do nothing and write nothing during it, thinking gets easier afterwards.

Ninety seconds. Walk out. Don't answer. Don't write. A minute and a half. The wave is physiologically short. At the peak your judgement is at its least reliable: thinking is least available at exactly that moment. A minute and a half later it comes back.

A physical pause. Stand up, leave the room, put the phone in your pocket. Physical action stops the flow of emotion more reliably than talking yourself down. Talking yourself down has to happen in thoughts, and your thoughts at that moment are already taken over by the emotion: they don't calm you, they supply new arguments for why everything is terrible. Your body isn't part of that flow, which is why the instruction to get up and leave gets through.

Name the emotion precisely. Not "I feel bad" but "I'm furious" or "I'm hurt". A precise word physically lowers the intensity: when you search for the name, the part of the cortex that handles words comes online and activity in the region that triggers alarm drops. There's more on this in the article on naming emotions, and eight states with their signs are in the emotions map.

Fact or interpretation. Split it into two columns. Fact: "she hasn't replied in three hours". Interpretation: "she thinks I'm incompetent". While the two are fused, you react to the interpretation as though it were reality. Splitting them returns the interpretation to the status of a guess, and reacting to a guess is far less compulsory.

Baseline checklist. When did you eat? Have you had water? How much did you sleep? On an empty stomach and short sleep the braking system works worse, and the same situation produces twice the reaction. Baseline first, content second, otherwise you're unpicking the consequences of a skipped lunch rather than the situation.

Time scale. Will this matter in a week? A month? A year? At the moment of reaction the scale collapses and what's happening feels like it defines your whole life. The question about the year puts the scale back.

When it doesn't work. If the emotion doesn't subside in a minute and a half and hasn't subsided an hour later, it isn't a wave. That's no longer about pause techniques, it's about what's going on in your life, and that's what needs looking at.

Fear of rejection and criticism

A separate group, though the skills are the same. This is about the painful reaction to rejection and criticism often labelled RSD, rejection sensitive dysphoria. It isn't an official diagnosis, but the term has stuck because it describes something recognisable.

From inside it doesn't feel like sensitivity, it feels like fact: I've been rejected, I'm not wanted, it's over. Which is exactly why reasoning doesn't get much traction on it. It doesn't arrive as an emotion, it arrives as knowledge.

A wins file. A note with concrete facts: what you did, what worked, who said what. Read it during an attack. In a threat state, attention runs narrow and pulls only confirmations of the threat out of memory. Anything good is physically unavailable at that moment, even though it exists. So it has to be lying outside as ready-made text rather than being searched for in your head.

Fact versus story. "He didn't reply" is a fact. "He doesn't care about me" is a story. Write both down side by side. Where data runs out, the brain fills in what's missing, and it fills in with the worst version. Writing it out shows where the data ended and the story began.

A code word. Agree with someone close on a word they say when they can see it's got you. From inside, the state isn't recognised as a state, it feels like the truth about what's happening, so you won't stop yourself. The signal has to come from outside. The word needs to be neutral and free of judgement.

Ninety seconds. Same as before: don't answer, don't write, wait it out.

When it doesn't work. If the criticism was real and fair, separating fact from invention is still worth doing, but you'll also have to accept the fact. A wins file isn't there to cancel feedback, it's there so feedback doesn't cancel you entirely.

Ashamed of myself

A swap happens here: behaviour turns into a verdict on your character. Forgot something, so I'm irresponsible. Didn't finish, so I never achieve anything.

Fact instead of verdict. "I forgot to send the email" is a fact, and you can do something with it. "I'm irresponsible" is a verdict. An action has a time, a place and a cause; a trait has none of those, so there's nothing to repair. The difference isn't tone, it's whether any possible action survives the sentence.

A done list. In the evening, write down three things you did. Every day. Undone things push themselves into view because they carry anxiety, while done things close and drop out of sight. Without the writing, your evening report only ever gets assembled from one column.

Do it messily. Start, send, hand it in as is. Putting things off "until I'm ready" often turns out to be fear of criticism wearing perfectionism as a costume: while the work hasn't been shown, it can't be judged, and there'll be no rejection. The price of that protection is that the work never comes out at all.

Don't compare directly. Comparing your process with someone else's result is invalid in principle: in yourself you see the drafts, the doubts and the three rewrites; in the other person you see only the finished thing. The comparison always comes out against you, because the two things aren't the same kind of thing.

When it doesn't work. A done list fails if you only put big things in it. Three things means literally three things, including "got to the post office" and "answered the email that had been sitting there a week".

No energy

The second half of this zone, and the mechanism here is different. It isn't about emotions, it's that resources come and go in waves while you plan as though they were level.

Productivity windows. Spend a week noting when your head works and when it doesn't. Then put demanding things into the windows and mechanical things into the troughs. The same task takes an hour in a window and three in a trough, and comes out worse. A schedule built without accounting for the wave is paid for with that difference every single day.

The cycle as a factor. Track the phases and don't schedule anything ambitious for the luteal phase. Hormone levels shift across the cycle, and with them how easily concentration comes and how much irritation you can absorb. It's a known fluctuation, and accounting for it is cheaper than fighting it.

Buffer time. Fifteen minutes of nothing between tasks. Each switch costs resources in itself, even if both tasks are easy: your brain is still wrapping up the previous task for a while. Without pauses you pay that tax ten times in a row and end the day wrung out by the transitions rather than the work.

Scheduled rest. Put rest in the calendar as a task. It sounds absurd, but unscheduled rest feels like laziness, produces guilt and therefore doesn't happen: you end up neither working nor resting. An entry in the calendar turns it into a permitted activity.

When it doesn't work. If there's been no energy for weeks on end and no windows turn up at all, this isn't a planning question. That's a reason to see a doctor rather than to look for a better system.

Thoughts going in circles

A separate misery: a thought circles for hours, it feels like you're solving a problem, and the output is zero.

The action test. Can I do something about this right now? If yes, do it. If no, this isn't analysis, it's anxiety wearing analysis as a costume. Analysis ends in a conclusion; anxiety ends in another lap, because its purpose isn't to solve the problem but to keep it under observation.

Brain dump. Get it all onto paper and don't solve anything. Part of the circling isn't held up by the problem itself but by the fear of forgetting to think about it. Once everything is written down, that fear goes and your head lets go.

A timer on thinking. Ten minutes with a timer, then get up and leave. Forbidding yourself to think doesn't work, because the forbidding runs on the same resource that's already occupied. Permission with an appointed exit point does work: you may think, but the end is known in advance.

The body. A shower, a walk, a different room. You can't stop the loop by thinking, the loop is made of the same material. Physical action and new surroundings give the flow something else to work on, and the circle breaks without any effort of will.

Say it out loud. Call someone and warn them a stream is coming. In your head the thought circles in fragments and therefore feels endless. Said in full, it becomes finite, and usually halfway through the sentence you can already hear where the hole in it is.

When it doesn't work. If what's circling isn't a problem but anxiety with no object, the action test returns "no" and nothing changes. Then only the physical works: a shower, walking, movement.

What this zone doesn't fix

Your emotions won't get weaker. You won't have more energy.

All these skills do is insert a gap between the reaction and the act, and remove the requirement to be the same person every day.

This is exactly what the article on how a skill works is about: a skill that works doesn't demand resources at the moment you don't have any. A minute and a half of silence works precisely because a minute and a half's worth of energy is always there.
