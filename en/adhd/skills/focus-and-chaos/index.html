Focus and chaos

You're looking for your keys. On the way you remember there's something important you forgot to tell a colleague. You open your phone to message them, spot a text from a friend, reply, watch a couple of reels, and surface fifteen minutes later without having sent anything. The keys are still missing. The list of fifteen things has been sitting there since morning and there's no obvious place to start.

Each of these feels like a separate problem. They have one cause.

Anything left only in your head doesn't exist

A person can hold a limited number of things in mind at once. That isn't an ADHD thing, it's everyone: we all have a ceiling, and past it we start dropping what we were holding.

You've got two options. Keep it all in your head and hope you'll remember (spoiler: you won't). Or move some of it out into the world around you: write it down, set a reminder, put it somewhere visible, say it out loud to another person.

Researchers call the second option cognitive offloading, and the finding is this: what you move out into the world gets found again almost every time. What stays in your head goes missing more often the more things you're trying to hold at once.

The difference between people isn't whether there's a memory ceiling. Everyone has one. The difference is what gets in under it. An ADHD brain holds onto whatever it finds interesting without effort, and drops the entire boring administrative layer: deadlines, agreements, where the keys are.

Hence the rule for this whole zone: anything left only in your head doesn't exist.

Put something away in a closed drawer and it's gone. Agree to something verbally and there's no agreement. This isn't forgetfulness and it isn't irresponsibility. It's how your brain works.

Every skill in this zone does the same thing: it moves out into the world what won't stay in your head.

Getting distracted: attention moves out into the world

Attention doesn't hold itself up, it needs supports. And nearly all of those supports sit outside your head rather than in it: the setting, sound, the presence of another person, the physical impossibility of getting distracted.

Hence the skills in this group. An entry ritual: the same headphones, the same playlist, the same spot every time, so the setting itself tells your brain what's happening now. Body doubling: working alongside someone in silence, in person or on video, even if that person couldn't care less about your task. Phone in another room: removing the temptation is easier than resisting it.

Multitasking sits slightly apart: background music or a podcast under a monotonous task. It sounds like a contradiction, but for some people it isn't interference, it's support, because the background stimulation is what lets them stay on the main task.

When it doesn't work. If the task needs silence, the background gets in the way. If choosing the music takes twenty minutes, the entry ritual has turned into a way of not starting.

Forgetting everything: memory moves out into the world

This is where the rule applies most literally. Your head is a poor storage system, and trying to turn it into a good one never works.

Brain dump: everything that's circling goes into one list, dictated or written. It frees up not just memory but the background anxiety of mustn't-forget.

One system: one place for notes, delete the rest. This matters more than it sounds. Your brain gives up after the first failed search, and five systems work worse than one bad one.

Context in the note: not "Masha" but "Masha, the report, by Friday". A note without context is useless; you'll find it and have no idea why you wrote it.

Saying it back out loud: "so what you want is for me to...". It moves what you heard from passive memory into active, and catches half the misunderstandings on the spot.

When it doesn't work. If your capture system takes five steps, you won't use it. Good external memory is whatever a thought reaches in three seconds.

Everything at once: priority moves out into the world

When fifteen tasks are sitting in your head in one heap, they all feel equally urgent. That isn't a failure of judgement, it's the absence of sorting: until the list is out on paper or on a screen, there's nothing to sort.

Three tasks: cross out everything except three. A short list doesn't overwhelm; a long one paralyses.

Must / Should / Could: three buckets instead of a hierarchy. Must falls apart today. Should becomes a problem next week. Could, well, whatever.

The "what burns first" question: what falls apart if it isn't done today? That's your first item. It works better than abstract importance, because the threat of losing something starts you moving more reliably than the promise of gaining something.

When it doesn't work. If planning takes an hour, it's become procrastination in a decent suit. Five minutes: open it, pick three, close it, start.

No sense of time: time moves out into the world

Your sense of time is another internal instrument, and it lies too. It lies systematically in one direction: every task looks shorter than it is.

Visual timer: not digits but a shrinking wedge you can see out of the corner of your eye. It replaces a broken internal metronome with an external one.

The times-two rule: multiply your estimate by two. Always. That isn't pessimism, it's a correction for a known systematic error, exactly like correcting for an instrument's margin of error.

Measurement: spend a week timing how long ordinary things actually take. Data calibrates estimates better than any amount of talking to yourself.

Backward planning: not "leave at one" but "be there at 14:00", then subtract the journey, getting ready, and a fifteen-minute buffer.

Mini-deadlines: a distant deadline doesn't exist as far as your brain is concerned, so a big task gets cut into pieces with deadlines a day or two out.

When it doesn't work. Measuring without writing it down does nothing: a week later you'll be remembering your old estimates rather than the real numbers.

Mess everywhere: where things live moves out into the world

If a thing isn't in sight, then as far as you're concerned it isn't there.

A home for it: one place for the keys, and only there, every single time. It works not because you remember the place but because your hand remembers the route.

Transparent and open: open baskets instead of closed drawers, clear containers instead of opaque ones. Invisible equals non-existent, and that isn't a metaphor, it's a description of how you'll be looking for that thing a month from now.

One function per surface: the desk is for work. Everything else comes off it, because mixing functions kills the association, and with it your ability to sit down there and start.

The thirty-second rule: if something takes less than thirty seconds, it gets done now. "Later" is the place things don't come back from.

When it doesn't work. If the home you picked is inconvenient, it won't stick. The place for the keys has to be where your hand goes by itself, not where it looks tidy.

What this zone doesn't fix

None of this adds resources. It stops them being wasted.

Your attention won't get stronger because the phone is in another room. It just stops going on resistance. Your memory won't improve because of a list. It just stops holding twenty things and dropping them one by one.

This is exactly what the article on how a skill works is about: a skill that works is built so that it doesn't demand resources at the moment you don't have any.

And the same test applies: does it work on a bad day? A capture system you only use when you're on a roll isn't a system.
