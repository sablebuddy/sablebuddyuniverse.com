🪤 Cognitive traps 16-20

A breakdown of five cognitive traps: how they work, how to spot them, what to do - and why the brain is wired this way at all.

Comparison, effort minimization, reverse telepathy, false dilemma, motive projection.

Context: Your brain is working to spec - where cognitive traps come from and why they are settings, not a malfunction.

Traps on this page:
ComparisonEffort MinimizationReverse TelepathyFalse DilemmaMotive Projection

16. Comparison

What it is

Constantly comparing yourself to 'normal' people

Example

She organizes her life easily, I can't even find my keys

What to do

Different architecture. You see her result, not her process. Not her brain.

Why your brain does this

Comparing yourself to others is how your brain figures out where you stand. The problem is, you're comparing your inner mess with someone else's polished surface. That math will never work.

17. Effort Minimization

What it is

'If it's hard for me, I must be doing it wrong'

Example

Others do this easily, but for me it takes everything I've got

What to do

Hard doesn't mean wrong. Your brain spends more resources on the same task.

Why your brain does this

Your brain has a simple rule: if something costs too much energy, you're probably doing it inefficiently. That rule works great for physical tasks. It's completely wrong for cognitive ones.

18. Reverse Telepathy

What it is

'They should have figured out what I need'

Example

He should have known I was hurt, without me saying anything

What to do

Say it directly. People aren't telepaths. A clear request isn't weakness.

Why your brain does this

Your brain assumes that what's obvious to you is obvious to everyone. You feel your emotions so intensely that it seems impossible others don't notice. But they don't. They're busy running their own brain.

19. False Dilemma

What it is

'Either I do this perfectly right now, or not at all'

Example

No time for a full workout - so no point going at all

What to do

15 minutes > 0 minutes. 70% > 0%. Messy > nothing.

Why your brain does this

Your brain pairs all-or-nothing thinking with a need for control. If you can't guarantee a great outcome, it would rather not play at all. That way you avoid both uncertainty and the chance of falling short.

20. Motive Projection

What it is

'They did this on purpose to hurt me'

Example

She didn't invite me - she's deliberately excluding me

What to do

Malice or carelessness? What's more likely? Ask directly.

Why your brain does this

When your brain can't explain someone's behavior, it defaults to threat: 'I don't know why they did that, so I'll assume the worst.' It's an ancient safety mechanism. Useful in the wild. Exhausting in the office.
